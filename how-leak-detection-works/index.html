How Water Leak Detection Works

Leak detection is a process of narrowing possibilities, not one magical scan. The most useful first question is what system is likely losing water. A professional may use several tests in sequence to establish a plausible leak source without making unnecessary openings.

Updated November 2026 · Practical homeowner guide

Which leak detection test should be used first?

The first test depends on the suspected system. A water meter and fixture-isolation check can show unexpected consumption; a supply-line pressure test can help assess a pressurized pipe; acoustic tools may narrow a noisy leak. Video cameras are mainly used to examine accessible drain or sewer interiors, not as a universal test for pressurized water pipes.

Source and reference: Sacramento County meter guidance
Fixture and meter checks

Useful for sorting out water use versus a suspected supply-line leak.

Pressure and acoustic tests

Useful for pressurized lines when conditions and access allow.

Camera and tracer methods

Selected for appropriate pipes or systems; a responsible report should note limitations.

Tracer gas, video inspections and electronic pipe tracing

Tracer gas

A specialist may use a compatible tracer-gas method on an isolated system to investigate certain difficult-to-locate leaks. It is not appropriate for every pipe or property.

Drain camera inspection

A camera can show accessible interior conditions in drain or sewer pipes. It cannot by itself prove that a pressurized supply line is leaking.

Electronic line tracing

Line tracing can help follow an underground pipe route. Locating the pipe is not the same thing as detecting the point where water escapes.

Ask the inspector which system is being tested, what the chosen method can establish, and what evidence would justify opening a wall, deck or slab.

Common diagnostic methods

  • Meter and fixture isolation — helps separate household use from continuous unexpected flow
  • Pressure testing — can indicate whether an isolated plumbing section holds pressure
  • Acoustic listening — may identify sound produced by escaping pressurized water
  • Thermal imaging — shows temperature differences; it does not see behind a wall
  • Moisture mapping — tracks damp material and helps define the affected area

What good findings should explain

  • Which line or area was tested
  • What evidence supports the suspected source
  • What remains uncertain and what additional access may be necessary
  • Whether the next step is testing, repair, drying or a different specialist
  • What is included in any written quote or report

Questions people ask

Is electronic leak detection non-invasive?+

Many initial techniques avoid opening surfaces, but not every leak can be confirmed without access.

Can thermal imaging detect water by itself?+

No. Temperature patterns can indicate conditions associated with moisture but need interpretation and corroboration.

What if the inspection finds no leak?+

Findings should identify what was tested and what remains possible; water loss can be intermittent or unrelated to pressurized piping.
